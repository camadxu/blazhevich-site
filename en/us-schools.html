American Trombone Schools

Teachers, orchestras, conservatories

This is not a single technique, and it is not a contest between cities. It is a network of teachers, orchestras, and conservatories. Singing, breathing, ensemble playing, and repertoire work show up in different traditions, and the boundaries between them are porous: Remington in Rochester held up the phrasing of Gardell Simons of Philadelphia as a model, and Alessi studied with Dodson at Curtis before he took charge of the trombones of the New York Philharmonic.

Divisions

Six addresses where the tradition takes institutional form

The order runs from schools to a city that has no school of its own: what follows is not a taxonomy of technique but a map of places.

  1. Eastman and Rochester. Emory Remington’s class (1922–1971) and the Trombone Choir he founded both sit inside the Eastman School of Music; the choir passed to Donald Knaub, George Osborn, and John Marcellus (1978–2014) and is now led by Larry Zalkind and Mark Kellogg.[1][4]
  2. Chicago. The Chicago Symphony’s working players: Kleinhammer never taught at a conservatory and ran a private studio; Jacobs taught tuba at Northwestern for more than twenty years; Vernon teaches at DePaul University. The tradition runs through an orchestra, a private studio, and two universities at once.[5][6][11]
  3. Philadelphia and Curtis. Curtis runs the narrowest studio on this list: up to two trombonists a year and 110 graduates in its whole history. Its first teacher was principal trombone of the Philadelphia Orchestra; Haroz, Vaughn, and Bollinger teach the class today.[12][13]
  4. New York and Juilliard. The class belongs to Joseph Alessi, principal trombone of the New York Philharmonic since 1985. Bass trombone sits in a separate program run by Bollinger and Rojak, and Per Brevig is the department’s historical figure.[16][18]
  5. Northwestern. Michael Mulcahy has headed the class at the Bienen School of Music since 1999; the department is staffed by working orchestral players, and Reed Capshaw directs the Trombone Choir.[20][21]
  6. Boston. No school here, only an entry point for the sheet music: from 1926 Jacob Raichman brought Blazhevich’s materials and worked from them with students, and the first American editions appeared here in 1939. The sources name neither a class nor a list of students — the “In America” page takes that up.

Blazhevich in America

Recognition in America

Blazhevich himself never went to America: it was his music that crossed the ocean.

How the music reached Boston

Trombonist Jacob Raichman played with Blazhevich in the Bolshoi Theatre orchestra and studied with him for a time. Leaving Russia in 1926, he took with him the 1925 method, the 26 Sequences, the Concert Duets, the Legato School and Concerto No. 9. In Boston he taught from these and wrote to Blazhevich for more. His letters from Boston of 10 November 1927 and 19 April 1932 survive in Blazhevich’s archive at the Glinka Museum of Musical Culture.[3]

The first American editions of Blazhevich were Concerto No. 2 (Cundy-Bettoney, Boston, 1939) and Concert Piece No. 5 (Belwin, 1939), the latter a year after the Russian edition.[3] According to Kharlamov, neither appeared with the author’s permission.[3] Earlier, in 1936, the 1936 School of Playing was published in the US under the editorship of Lewis Van Haney — the first American publication, often confused with the school in clefs.[3] In 1948 the New York publisher Leeds Music issued the 1925 method under Raichman’s editorship under the new title “Clef Studies for Trombone”. According to Kharlamov, Raichman broke a promise not to share the materials — Leeds soon published the “26 Sequences” and the “Legato School” in his edition.[3]

Why American players rate him

Blazhevich’s etudes run from a first encounter with the tenor and alto clefs to tasks for professionals. Caleb Lambert places him alongside the most influential writers for brass: Arban, Bordogni, Müller, Schlossberg and Kopprasch.[6] A reviewer for the International Trombone Association calls his etudes a “constant staple” of the teaching repertoire. American teachers even publish introductory collections subtitled “Before Blazhevich” — Brad Edwards’s 2002 book prepares students for the second part of the Clef Studies.[10] Concert Piece No. 5 became a required competition piece at many American summer music camps.[3]

There is a darker side to this story. According to Kharlamov and English Wikipedia, many American editions appeared without permission and without royalties to the heirs, and the texts were heavily re-edited. In 2007 East-West International began issuing verified editions based on the originals, with the family’s consent and with royalties. It was founded by the trombonist Andrey Kharlamov, who himself travelled to Russia looking for the heirs. According to the ITA reviewer, Kharlamov secured the heirs’ rights on the strength of the GATT trade agreement, and the family now receives royalties.[2][3][10]

Jacob Abramovich Raichman (Jacob Raichman)

St. Petersburg → Moscow → Boston
1892–1982

Childhood. Born in Vitebsk, now Belarus. As a boy he played the cello. His father was a bandmaster in the Russian army and advised his son to switch to the trombone.[4]

St. Petersburg. Raichman graduated from the St. Petersburg Conservatory in 1911 in the class of P. N. Volkov. The inscription on his trombone case reads: “The Schubert Prize to J. A. Raichman, who completed the St. Petersburg Conservatory in the class of P. N. Volkov, 1911.” The silver instrument itself, the work of the German maker Heckel, was the graduation award.[4]

Moscow. He then moved to Moscow, played in the Bolshoi Theatre orchestra, met Blazhevich and studied with him for a time. The violinist Artur Shtilman calls Raichman “an outstanding trombonist” of the pre-revolutionary Bolshoi.[4][9]

The road to America. Serge Koussevitzky, himself a former Bolshoi double-bassist, took Raichman into the Boston Symphony Orchestra — according to his daughter, even without an audition. The immigration quota for Russians was full, so in January 1927 Raichman arrived from Havana aboard the steamship Orizaba. Later, because of document trouble, he had to return to Cuba, and in December 1929 he entered the United States again, with his wife Theresa and three-month-old daughter Irina. He became a US citizen in 1937. He often signed as Jacques.[4]

Boston. In the 1927–28 season Raichman was the fourth of five trombonists; in 1928 he became co-principal alongside the Frenchman Joannès Rochut, and in 1929 he received a raise while Rochut’s pay was cut. After Rochut left in 1930, Raichman was principal trombone until 1955; his successor was William Gibson. He soloed in Ravel’s Bolero in the 1937 (Boston Pops) and 1947 recordings.[4]

Koussevitzky. The Library of Congress holds four of his letters to Koussevitzky in Russian. Three letters of 1930 concern a visa; the letter of 1945 ends “remaining forever devoted to you.”[4]

The bridge for Blazhevich. In 1948 Raichman published Blazhevich’s school in clefs under the title Clef Studies for Trombone. His archive, given by the family to Boston University, partly perished in a water leak, according to Kharlamov.[3][4]

Eastman: Emory Remington and the Trombone Choir

Emory Remington (1891 or 1892 – December 10, 1971) taught the trombone class at the Eastman School of Music in Rochester from 1922 to 1971, nearly half a century; Brass Bulletin dates the start to 1921, the year the school was founded. His students called him The Chief.[1][2] The sources disagree on the year of his birth: Brass Bulletin (1978), the ITA Newsletter (1980), and a University of North Texas dissertation (2002) give 1891, Eastman’s official page and Wikipedia give 1892. One indirect argument for 1891: his centenary was formally celebrated in 1991.

The method is called the “singing school.” Remington wrote almost nothing; the technique passed by voice and demonstration, and the student took the sound from the teacher’s singing. His own formula: “If I had a special distinction, it was that I tried to make the trombone sing with a human quality… I didn’t blow into the horn, I sang into it.” The warm-up was built from long tones, a soft attack, flexibility on overtone slurs, breathing in legato, and scale “patterns” in every key.[2][3][29]

He also founded the Eastman Trombone Choir; its first concert was in November 1941. Students made their own arrangements of chorales by Bach and Handel. Donald Knaub led the choir (1972–1977, or until Marcellus arrived in 1978), then George Osborn, and from 1978 John Marcellus, for 36 years; Mark Kellogg and Larry Zalkind are its co-directors today.[4]

Estimates of the school’s size differ: Brass Bulletin says more than a thousand students over three generations, Eastman says “hundreds.”[2][1]

The sources follow the choir’s succession more closely than the class’s: they do not establish who taught the trombone class after Remington.

Chicago: Arnold Jacobs, “Song and Wind,” Kleinhammer, Vernon

The Chicago tradition formed around two men who spent decades in the same orchestra. Bass trombonist Edward Kleinhammer played in the Chicago Symphony from 1940 through June 1985 — 45 years and seven music directors — and tubist Arnold Jacobs from 1944 to 1988.[5][6] Wikipedia’s opening sentence on Kleinhammer says “1940–1983,” which contradicts the article’s own text about 45 years and the orchestra’s archive: the correct date is June 1985.[7][5]

Jacobs’s formula splits attention roughly 85 to 15: song is the musical image in the head, wind the breath that sets the lips moving. At a 1995 appearance he said: “we never play a brass instrument by air, we play by Song.” His assistant Brian Frederiksen assembled the book about it; WindSong Press published it in 1996.[8][9]

Kleinhammer wrote “The Art of Trombone Playing” (Summy-Birchard, 1963), devised the removable E attachment that Holton manufactured, and ran a private studio in Chicago’s Fine Arts Building instead of teaching at a conservatory.[10][7][28] Charles Vernon, bass trombone of the Chicago Symphony since 1986, studied with both men, teaches at DePaul University, and wrote “The Singing Trombone”; he sets out his method through Jacobs — “playing a brass instrument as being song and wind.”[11]

Philadelphia and Curtis

The Curtis Institute of Music opened on October 13, 1924; its founder was Mary Louise Curtis Bok, and students faced no financial barrier to study there.[12] Gardell Simons taught trombone first (1924–1930) — the same man whose phrasing Remington called exemplary. Paul Lotz took the class for the 1925–1926 season, and Charles Gerhard from 1931 to 1942.[13][3]

The studio is tiny: at most two trombonists a year, 110 graduates in its whole history, the first of them Edward Challenger (1927). Charles Gusikoff, Henry Charles Smith III, M. Dee Stewart, Glenn Dodson, and Charles Vernon taught the class from 1947; Nitzan Haroz, Matthew Vaughn, and Blair Bollinger teach it today.[13] Curtis’s own text says Dodson taught “nearly 30 years,” but the school’s chronology gives 1969–1988 — 19 years.[13]

Curtis produced Joseph Alessi (’81) and Blair Bollinger (’86), bass trombonist of the Philadelphia Orchestra since 1986.[13][15] Blazhevich appears outright in Curtis’s entrance requirements: a technical etude of the candidate’s choice — Kopprasch, Tyrell, or Blazhevich — and a lyrical one — Bordogni or Rochut — plus reading in alto, tenor, and bass clefs.[14]

New York: Joseph Alessi

Joseph Alessi became principal trombone of the New York Philharmonic in the spring of 1985. Before that he spent four seasons as second trombone in Philadelphia and one as principal in Montreal; he studied at Curtis and graduated in 1981.[16][17] Since 1986 he has taught at Juilliard, and since 2007 at its Pre-College.[16]

He made his solo debut with the orchestra in April 1990, in Creston’s Fantasy for trombone. World premieres of concertos by Christopher Rouse (1992, for the orchestra’s 150th anniversary), Melinda Wagner (2007), and William Bolcom (2016) followed; in 2002 the International Trombone Association gave him its award.[16]

Juilliard runs tenor and bass trombone as separate programs (BM, MM, GD, DMA): the bass instrument is not a specialization inside the tenor class. Blair Bollinger and John D. Rojak, a pupil of Don Harwood, lead the bass side; Per Brevig, principal trombone at the Metropolitan Opera from 1968 to 1994, is the department’s historical figure.[18][15] On his Resources page Alessi sets the work out in order: technique, etudes, solo literature, orchestral excerpts. The same page carries his cadenzas to the first two movements of Albrechtsberger’s concerto for alto trombone, written with Norman Stumpf.[19]

Northwestern: Michael Mulcahy

Michael Mulcahy joined the Chicago Symphony in 1989 at Georg Solti’s invitation; before that he worked in Australia and Germany. In 1999 he took over the trombone class at the Bienen School of Music at Northwestern University.[20]

The department is staffed by working orchestral players: Randall Hawes, Timothy Higgins, Mulcahy, and R. Douglas Wright teach trombone; Reed Capshaw directs the Trombone Choir; Gene Pokorny and Matthew Gaunt teach tuba.[21] Arnold Jacobs taught tuba here for more than twenty years — the same Chicago lineage.[6]

The link to Blazhevich is direct: Mulcahy took part in preparing the “Sequences” for the East-West International editions of 2007–2012.[23] The university’s own biography, though, records a career rather than a special “Northwestern method”: a school cannot be derived from a text about a teacher. Mulcahy, Hawes, and Wright sit on the jury of the competition named after Kleinhammer (ITA, under 25) and appear among its finalists.[22]

What the Schools Share and How They Differ

Who leads the class. In the United States the norm is a working orchestral player. Curtis had the principal trombonist of the Philadelphia Orchestra in charge from its founding; Juilliard has the principal trombone of the New York Philharmonic and the bass trombonist of Philadelphia; Northwestern has four orchestral players. Eastman falls outside the pattern: the sources describe Remington’s class and his choir in detail, but not his own orchestral service. Kleinhammer is the second exception: an orchestral player, but never a conservatory teacher, so his school exists as a private studio. Moscow and Leningrad follow the same norm: Bork, Blazhevich, Shcherbinin, Grigoriev, Volkov, Kozlov, and Venglovsky are orchestra and theatre people, not theorists working apart. On this axis the two countries do not divide.

Method: spoken or written. Remington left no written course: the technique was passed on by voice and demonstration, and of printed material only the “Remington Warm-Up Studies” remain (1980; second edition).[29] Kleinhammer does have a book — “The Art of Trombone Playing” (1963). Jacobs did not write his own book — Brian Frederiksen assembled it (1996). Vernon’s is “The Singing Trombone.”[10][9] Russia looks just as mixed: Shcherbinin left no printed school, and his legacy is a “special system of daily practice” and the living practice of the class; Türner has two printed schools (1877 and 1878), Venglovsky has “Daily Exercises for Trombone.” The difference the material does show is this: American books are personal textbooks by individual performers, while Russian methods more often grow out of a class and its continuity. The sources stop at that formulation: they contain no curricula and no syllabi.

The role of the ensemble. Eastman is built around a standing ensemble: Remington founded the Trombone Choir, it gave its first concert in November 1941, and the students made their own arrangements of chorales by Bach and Handel. At Northwestern Reed Capshaw directs the Trombone Choir. For Curtis, Juilliard, and the Chicago lineage the sources name no such ensemble. In Leningrad the ensemble is a quartet, and it too grows out of the class: Kozlov’s pupils formed the core of the Philharmonic Trombone Quartet. The resemblance is exact and incomplete at once: in both places a standing ensemble is part of the training, and the two differ in ownership — Eastman’s choir belongs to the school, the quartet to the philharmonic.

What counts as a result. The sources never state the aims of a school; all that shows is what they count as an achievement. For Eastman it is scale: “more than a thousand students over three generations” and “hundreds.” For Curtis it is small numbers and the precision of the match: 110 graduates in its whole history, with Alessi and Bollinger named. For Juilliard it is a solo career: a debut with orchestra in 1990, world premieres, the ITA prize of 2002. For the Chicago lineage it is the continuity of the bass trombone: Vernon studied with Jacobs and Kleinhammer. In Moscow and Leningrad competition prizes and an orchestral post come first. A direct comparison is impossible here: a competition prize and a solo premiere are different measures, and the sources give no common scale.

The relation to Blazhevich. The spread could hardly be wider. At Curtis his etude appears in the audition requirements alongside Kopprasch and Tyrell.[14] At Northwestern his name turns up in the work itself: Mulcahy took part in preparing the “Sequences” for the East-West International editions.[23] For Eastman, the Chicago lineage, and Juilliard his name does not occur in the sources at all — which does not mean he is absent, only that the sources are silent. In Moscow he is the foundation of the class; in Leningrad, the repertoire of the department. Without him the lineage is described less fully.

The borders are porous. A school almost always means a teacher’s name and the circle of his pupils, not a city. Bollinger teaches at Curtis and at Juilliard at once; Raichman graduated from the St. Petersburg Conservatory under Volkov, played at the Bolshoi, studied with Blazhevich, and took his scores to Boston; Vernon studied with the tubist and the bass trombonist of a single orchestral section. The city is only where the circle became an institution.

Analogies with Moscow and Leningrad

Moscow: the theatre and the class. The Moscow lineage is theatrical in its organization: Bork came to the conservatory class from the orchestra of the Bolshoi Theatre, and the same pattern runs on through Blazhevich, Shcherbinin, and Grigoriev. The class sits inside the conservatory but depends on theatre people. The resemblance to Chicago is direct: the method goes from orchestral player to pupil by voice and demonstration rather than through an institution, and Kleinhammer’s private studio in Chicago’s Fine Arts Building is the closest American analogue to the Moscow class attached to a theatre. The two differ in how the pattern is fixed: in Moscow the same scheme is written into a conservatory, with programs and titles; in Chicago it exists with no conservatory at all.

Leningrad: discipline and ensemble. The St. Petersburg lineage is orchestral in its organization: Volkov was principal trombone at the Mariinsky Theatre, and the “Volkov method” grew out of Russian operatic singing; Kozlov played in the philharmonic orchestra for more than half a century, and his pupils made up the core of the quartet. The resemblance to Eastman lies in the role of the ensemble: in both places a standing ensemble is part of the training and the platform where the repertoire is tested.

What separates them is the address. In the United States a school is housed in a university: the Eastman School of Music, the Bienen School at Northwestern, Juilliard with its Pre-College, Curtis with instruction free of financial barriers. The programs are described, and at Juilliard bass trombone is a separate degree. In Russia it is housed in a conservatory and a theatre: the Moscow Conservatory and the Bolshoi Theatre, the St. Petersburg Conservatory and the Mariinsky with the philharmonic, the Gnesin branch as a parallel channel. Hence the difference in the biographies: the American sources show moves between cities (Mulcahy — Australia, Germany, Chicago), while in the Moscow and Leningrad material every post stays in one city and in two or three connected institutions.

Where the analogy ends. The material offers almost no technical parallels. The one documented difference inside the Russian tradition is legato: according to HBS, the Moscow school plays it without tongue and throat, the Leningrad school with the possible involvement of the tongue. For the American schools the sources have no comparable norm: Remington’s and Jacobs’s formulas are about singing and breathing, not about articulation. The analogies above are therefore structural, not technical.

Research, Articles, and Sheet Music

Some of this literature is open access, the rest is for sale. The open-access side includes Jacobs’s 1973 lecture at Indiana, the dissertations of John LeBlanc (Manhattan School of Music, 2000) and Jonathan Rowsell (Toronto, 2018), Caleb Lambert’s study of Blazhevich’s solo works (Iowa, 2020), and Gregory Hinterbichler’s study of the evolving role of the solo trombone in the nineteenth century (UNT); the publisher has also posted the Kleinhammer chapter from the 1996 book as a PDF.[24][25][8][26][32][28] Still for sale and under copyright: “Song and Wind” (1996), “The Art of Trombone Playing” (1963), “The Remington Warm-Up Studies” (1980; second edition), and “The Singing Trombone.” No free copies of these editions exist.[9][10][29]

Open-Access Sheet Music

  • Blazhevich on IMSLP: “School for Slide Trombone” (Muzgiz, 1925), “Sequences,” the concert duets, and 70 etudes for tuba or bass trombone.[27]
  • IMSLP also carries Beethoven’s 3 Equali, early editions of the Kopprasch etudes, Vobaron (1857), Cornette (about 1854), Jean White (1885), and Bordogni’s vocalises.[27]
  • Rochut’s “Melodious Etudes” is his own adaptation of Bordogni’s vocalises and is not in the public domain; Lafosse (1928) is marked PD under U.S. law only.

What to Listen To

  • Caleb Lambert’s project: the first four Blazhevich concertos, recorded for his 2020 dissertation.[30]
  • Verified sources turned up no open-access recordings by Remington, Jacobs, Kleinhammer, or Vernon – the section will grow.

Limits of the comparison: no source describes the student ensembles at Curtis, Juilliard, Chicago, or the Moscow class; no comparable student numbers exist for Chicago, Northwestern, or Juilliard; no source sets out technical standards for the American schools the way the sources record a difference in legato between Moscow and Leningrad; audition requirements are documented only for Curtis; no source draws a causal line from “school → orchestral position.” What remains unconfirmed: Charles Vernon’s year of birth; the absence of any public-domain portrait of Remington, Jacobs, Kleinhammer, or Vernon; the exact years George Osborn directed the Eastman Trombone Choir; the year and ISBN of “The Singing Trombone,” which the publisher does not give; the full texts of Hunsberger (1980) and Reynolds (1991) and the Colegrove (1999) dissertation, none of which has turned up in open access.
