American Trombone Schools

Teachers, orchestras, conservatories

This is not a single technique or a contest between cities but a network of teachers, orchestras, and conservatories. Singing, breathing, ensemble playing, and work on repertoire turn up in different traditions, and the boundaries between them are porous: Remington in Rochester called the phrasing of Gardell Simons of Philadelphia a model, and Alessi studied with Dodson at Curtis before he took over the trombones of the New York Philharmonic.

Divisions

Six addresses where the tradition has an organizational body

The order runs from a school to a city where there is no school as such: the division below is not a taxonomy of technique but a map of places.

  1. Eastman and Rochester. Held by the Eastman School of Music: Emory Remington’s class (1922–1971) and the Trombone Choir he founded, which after him was led by Donald Knaub, George Osborn, and John Marcellus (1978–2014), and which is now led by Larry Zalkind and Mark Kellogg.[1][4]
  2. Chicago. Held by working members of the Chicago Symphony: Kleinhammer did not teach at a conservatory and ran a private studio, Jacobs taught tuba at Northwestern for more than twenty years, and Vernon teaches at DePaul University. The line lives in the orchestra, the private studio, and two universities at once.[5][6][11]
  3. Philadelphia and Curtis. Held by the Curtis Institute, with the smallest studio of those listed: up to two trombonists a year and 110 graduates over its history. The first teacher was principal trombone of the Philadelphia Orchestra; today the class is taught by Haroz, Vaughn, and Bollinger.[12][13]
  4. New York and Juilliard. Held by Joseph Alessi, principal trombone of the New York Philharmonic since 1985; bass trombone is set apart in a separate program, run by Bollinger and Rojak, and the historical figure of the department is Per Brevig.[16][18]
  5. Northwestern. Held by the Bienen School of Music: since 1999 Michael Mulcahy has headed the class, the department is made up of working orchestral players, and Reed Capshaw directs the Trombone Choir.[20][21]
  6. Boston. There is no school here — there is an entry point for the sheet music: from 1926 Jacob Raichman brought Blazhevich’s materials and worked from them with students, and the first American editions of 1939 came out here as well. The sources name neither a class nor a list of students — that is on the “In America” page.

Eastman: Emory Remington and the Trombone Choir

Emory Remington (1891 or 1892 – December 10, 1971) taught the trombone class at the Eastman School of Music in Rochester from 1922 to 1971 — nearly half a century; Brass Bulletin gives 1921, the year the school was founded. His students called him The Chief.[1][2] The year of his birth differs among the sources: Brass Bulletin (1978), the ITA Newsletter (1980), and a University of North Texas dissertation (2002) give 1891, while Eastman’s official page and Wikipedia give 1892. An indirect argument in favor of 1891 is that Remington’s centenary was celebrated formally in 1991.

The method is called the “singing school”: Remington wrote almost nothing, the technique was passed on by voice and demonstration, and the student took the sound from the teacher’s singing. His own formula: “If I had a special distinction, it was that I tried to make the trombone sing with a human quality… I didn’t blow into the horn, I sang into it.” The warm-up consisted of long tones, a soft attack, flexibility on overtone slurs, breathing in legato, and scale “patterns” in every key.[2][3][29]

The Eastman Trombone Choir was founded by him as well; its first concert was in November 1941. Students made their own arrangements of chorales by Bach and Handel. The choir was led by Donald Knaub (1972–1977, or until Marcellus arrived in 1978), George Osborn, and from 1978 by John Marcellus, for 36 years; today its co-directors are Mark Kellogg and Larry Zalkind.[4]

The scale of the school is assessed differently: Brass Bulletin speaks of more than a thousand students over three generations, Eastman of “hundreds.”[2][1]

The sources record the continuity of the choir more precisely than the continuity of the class: who taught the trombone class after Remington does not follow from them.

Chicago: Arnold Jacobs, “Song and Wind,” Kleinhammer, Vernon

The Chicago line formed around a pair who worked in the same orchestra for decades. Bass trombonist Edward Kleinhammer played in the Chicago Symphony from 1940 through June 1985 — 45 years and seven music directors; tubist Arnold Jacobs from 1944 to 1988.[5][6] The lead of Wikipedia’s article on Kleinhammer says “1940–1983,” which contradicts the article’s own text about 45 years and the orchestra’s archive: the correct date is June 1985.[7][5]

Jacobs’s formula divides attention roughly 85 to 15: song is the musical image in the head, wind is the breath that sets the lips in motion. At a 1995 appearance he said: “we never play a brass instrument by air, we play by Song.” The book about it was put together by his assistant Brian Frederiksen; WindSong Press published it in 1996.[8][9]

Kleinhammer wrote “The Art of Trombone Playing” (Summy-Birchard, 1963), devised the removable E attachment that Holton manufactured, and, without teaching at a conservatory, ran a private studio in Chicago’s Fine Arts Building.[10][7][28] Charles Vernon, bass trombone of the Chicago Symphony since 1986, studied with both of them, teaches at DePaul University, and wrote “The Singing Trombone”; he formulates his method through Jacobs — “playing a brass instrument as being song and wind.”[11]

Philadelphia and Curtis

The Curtis Institute of Music opened on October 13, 1924; its founder was Mary Louise Curtis Bok, with instruction free of financial barriers for students.[12] The first trombone teacher was Gardell Simons (1924–1930) — the very man whose phrasing Remington called exemplary; Paul Lotz taught the class for the 1925–1926 season, and Charles Gerhard from 1931 to 1942.[13][3]

The studio is tiny: up to two trombonists a year, 110 graduates over its whole history, the first of them Edward Challenger (1927). From 1947 the class had Charles Gusikoff, Henry Charles Smith III, M. Dee Stewart, Glenn Dodson, and Charles Vernon; today it has Nitzan Haroz, Matthew Vaughn, and Blair Bollinger.[13] Curtis’s official text says that Dodson taught “nearly 30 years,” but the school’s chronology gives 1969–1988, that is, 19 years.[13]

Joseph Alessi (’81) and Blair Bollinger (’86), bass trombonist of the Philadelphia Orchestra since 1986, came from here.[13][15] Blazhevich is part of Curtis’s entrance requirements directly: a technical etude of the candidate’s choice — Kopprasch, Tyrell, or Blazhevich — a lyrical one — Bordogni or Rochut — plus reading in alto, tenor, and bass clefs.[14]

New York: Joseph Alessi

Joseph Alessi was appointed principal trombone of the New York Philharmonic in the spring of 1985. Before that he played second trombone in Philadelphia for four seasons and principal in Montreal for one, and he studied at Curtis, which he graduated from in 1981.[16][17] He has taught at Juilliard since 1986 and at the Pre-College since 2007.[16]

His solo debut with the orchestra came in April 1990, with Creston’s Fantasy for trombone; after that came world premieres of concertos by Christopher Rouse (1992, for the orchestra’s 150th anniversary), Melinda Wagner (2007), and William Bolcom (2016). In 2002 he was awarded the prize of the International Trombone Association.[16]

Juilliard keeps tenor and bass trombone in separate programs (BM, MM, GD, DMA): bass is not a specialization within the tenor class. The bass wing is led by Blair Bollinger and John D. Rojak, a pupil of Don Harwood; the historical figure of the department is Per Brevig, principal trombone of the Metropolitan Opera from 1968 to 1994.[18][15] On his Resources page Alessi sets out the work in order: technique, etudes, solo literature, orchestral excerpts; the same page publishes his cadenzas to the first two movements of Albrechtsberger’s concerto for alto trombone, written together with Norman Stumpf.[19]

Northwestern: Michael Mulcahy

Michael Mulcahy joined the Chicago Symphony in 1989 at the invitation of Georg Solti, and before Chicago he worked in Australia and Germany. In 1999 he took over the trombone class at the Bienen School of Music of Northwestern University.[20]

The department rests on working orchestral players: trombone is taught by Randall Hawes, Timothy Higgins, Mulcahy, and R. Douglas Wright; the Trombone Choir is directed by Reed Capshaw, and tuba is taught by Gene Pokorny and Matthew Gaunt.[21] Arnold Jacobs taught tuba here for more than twenty years — the same Chicago line.[6]

The link to Blazhevich is direct: in the East-West International editions of 2007–2012 the “Sequences” were prepared with Mulcahy’s participation.[23] At the same time, the university biography records a career rather than a special “Northwestern method”: a school cannot be derived from a text about a teacher. On the jury and among the finalists of the competition named after Kleinhammer (ITA, under 25) are Mulcahy, Hawes, and Wright.[22]

What the Schools Share and How They Differ

Who leads the class. In the United States the norm is a working orchestral player: at Curtis the class was led from its founding by the principal trombonist of the Philadelphia Orchestra, at Juilliard it is led by the principal trombone of the New York Philharmonic and the bass trombonist of Philadelphia, at Northwestern by four orchestral players. Eastman falls out of this pattern: the sources describe Remington’s class and his choir in detail, but not his own orchestral service. The second exception is Kleinhammer: he was an orchestral player, but he did not teach at a conservatory, and his school exists as a private studio. In Moscow and Leningrad the norm is the same: Bork, Blazhevich, Shcherbinin, Grigoriev, Volkov, Kozlov, and Venglovsky are people of the orchestra and the theatre, not separate theorists. This axis does not divide the countries.

Method: spoken or written. Remington has no written course — the technique was passed on by voice and demonstration; of printed material the “Remington Warm-Up Studies” remain (1980; second edition).[29] Kleinhammer does have a book — “The Art of Trombone Playing” (1963); Jacobs’s book was not written by him but assembled by Brian Frederiksen (1996); Vernon’s is “The Singing Trombone.”[10][9] In Russia the picture is just as mixed: Shcherbinin has no printed school, and his legacy is a “special system of daily practice” and the living practice of the class; Türner has two printed schools (1877 and 1878), Venglovsky has “Daily Exercises for Trombone.” The difference the material does show is this: American books are personal tutors by particular performers, while Russian methods more often grow out of a class and its continuity. The sources go no further than this formulation: they contain no curricula or programs.

The role of the ensemble. Eastman rests on a standing ensemble: the Trombone Choir was founded by Remington, its first concert was in November 1941, and the students made their own arrangements of chorales by Bach and Handel; at Northwestern the Trombone Choir is directed by Reed Capshaw. For Curtis, Juilliard, and the Chicago line the sources name no teaching ensembles. In Leningrad the ensemble is a quartet, and it too grows out of the class: the core of the Philharmonic Trombone Quartet was made up of Kozlov’s pupils. The resemblance is exact but incomplete: in both places a standing ensemble is part of the training, and the difference lies in ownership — Eastman’s choir belongs to the school, the quartet to the philharmonic.

What counts as a result. The sources do not formulate the aims of a school; all that can be seen is what they list as an achievement. For Eastman it is scale: “more than a thousand students over three generations” and “hundreds.” For Curtis it is the small number and the precision of the match: 110 graduates over its history, of whom Alessi and Bollinger are named. For Juilliard it is a solo career: a debut with orchestra in 1990, world premieres, the ITA prize of 2002. For the Chicago line it is the continuity of the bass trombone: Vernon studied with Jacobs and Kleinhammer. In Moscow and Leningrad the first place belongs to competition prizes and an orchestral post. A direct comparison is impossible here: a competition prize and a solo premiere are different measures, and the sources give no common scale.

The relation to Blazhevich. The spread is extreme. At Curtis his etude stands in the audition requirements alongside Kopprasch and Tyrell.[14] At Northwestern his name appears in the work itself: the “Sequences” in the East-West International editions were prepared with Mulcahy’s participation.[23] For Eastman, the Chicago line, and Juilliard his name does not occur in the sources at all — that does not mean he is not there, it means he is not in the sources. In Moscow he is the foundation of the class, in Leningrad the repertoire of the department, and without him the line is described less fully.

The borders are porous. A school almost always means the name of a teacher and the circle of his pupils, not a city. Bollinger teaches classes at Curtis and at Juilliard at the same time; Raichman graduated from the St. Petersburg Conservatory under Volkov, played at the Bolshoi, studied with Blazhevich, and took his sheet music to Boston; Vernon studied with the tubist and the bass trombonist of one orchestral section. The city is only the place where the circle became established as an institution.

Analogies with Moscow and Leningrad

Moscow: the theatre and the class. The Moscow line is organized as a theatrical one: Bork came into the conservatory class from the orchestra of the Bolshoi Theatre, and the same scheme continues with Blazhevich, Shcherbinin, and Grigoriev. The class exists at the conservatory but rests on people of the theatre. The resemblance to Chicago is direct: the method passes from orchestral player to pupil by voice and demonstration rather than through an institution, and Kleinhammer’s private studio in Chicago’s Fine Arts Building is the closest American analogue of the Moscow class attached to a theatre. The difference is in how it is fixed: in Moscow the same scheme is written into a conservatory with programs and titles, in Chicago it exists without a conservatory at all.

Leningrad: discipline and ensemble. The St. Petersburg line is organized as an orchestral one: Volkov was principal trombone of the Mariinsky Theatre, and the “Volkov method” grew out of Russian operatic singing; Kozlov played in the philharmonic orchestra for more than half a century, and his pupils made up the core of the quartet. The resemblance to Eastman lies in the role of the ensemble: in both places a standing ensemble is part of the training and the platform on which the repertoire is tested.

The main difference is the address. In the United States a school lives inside a university: the Eastman School of Music, the Bienen School at Northwestern, Juilliard with its Pre-College, Curtis with instruction free of financial barriers; the programs are described, and bass trombone at Juilliard is a separate degree. In Russia a school lives inside a conservatory and a theatre: the Moscow Conservatory and the Bolshoi Theatre, the St. Petersburg Conservatory and the Mariinsky with the philharmonic, the Gnesin branch as a parallel channel. Hence the difference in biographies: the American sources show moves between cities (Mulcahy — Australia, Germany, Chicago), while in the Moscow and Leningrad ones every post is in a single city and in two or three connected institutions.

Where the analogy ends. The material offers almost no technical parallels. The only documented difference within the Russian tradition is legato: according to HBS, in the Moscow school it is played without tongue and throat, in the Leningrad school with the possible involvement of the tongue. For the American schools the sources have no comparable norm: Remington’s and Jacobs’s formulas are about singing and breathing, not about articulation. The analogies above are therefore drawn by structure, not by technique.

Research, Articles, and Sheet Music

Some of the literature is open access, some is for sale. Freely available are Jacobs’s 1973 lecture at Indiana, the dissertations of John LeBlanc (Manhattan School of Music, 2000) and Jonathan Rowsell (Toronto, 2018), and Caleb Lambert’s study of Blazhevich’s solo works (Iowa, 2020); the publisher has also posted the chapter on Kleinhammer from the 1996 book as a PDF.[24][25][8][26][28] Still on sale and under copyright are “Song and Wind” (1996), “The Art of Trombone Playing” (1963), “The Remington Warm-Up Studies” (1980; second edition), and “The Singing Trombone” — no free copies of these editions exist.[9][10][29]

Open-Access Sheet Music

  • Blazhevich on IMSLP: “School for Slide Trombone” (Muzgiz, 1925), “Sequences,” concert duets, 70 etudes for tuba or bass trombone.[27]
  • Also there: Beethoven’s 3 Equali, early editions of the Kopprasch etudes, Vobaron (1857), Cornette (about 1854), Jean White (1885), Bordogni’s vocalises.[27]
  • Rochut’s “Melodious Etudes” — his own adaptation of Bordogni’s vocalises — is not in the public domain; Lafosse (1928) is marked PD under U.S. law only.

What to Listen To

  • Caleb Lambert’s project: the first four Blazhevich concertos, recorded for the 2020 dissertation.[30]
  • No open-access recordings of Remington, Jacobs, Kleinhammer, or Vernon were found in verified sources — the section will grow.

Limits of the comparison: the sources do not describe the student ensembles at Curtis, Juilliard, Chicago, or the Moscow class; there are no comparable student numbers for Chicago, Northwestern, or Juilliard; technical standards of the American schools do not exist in the form in which a difference in legato is documented for Moscow and Leningrad; audition requirements are documented only for Curtis; the sources do not show a causal link from “school → orchestral position.” What remains unconfirmed: Charles Vernon’s year of birth; no public-domain portraits of Remington, Jacobs, Kleinhammer, or Vernon exist; the exact years George Osborn directed the Eastman Trombone Choir are not established; the publisher does not indicate the year or ISBN of “The Singing Trombone”; full texts of Hunsberger (1980), Reynolds (1991), and the Colegrove (1999) dissertation have not been found in open access.
